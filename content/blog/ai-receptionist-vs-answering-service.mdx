Your phone rings while you are on a ladder. You have four options for what happens next, and they are not close to equivalent.

I build one of these for a living, so read this with that in mind. I have tried to be fair to the alternatives, including where mine loses.

## Option 1: Voicemail

**Cost:** free. **What it does:** records a message. **What it books:** nothing.

Voicemail made sense when the caller had no alternative. If they wanted a plumber in 1994, they left a message and waited, because the next plumber in the phone book also had an answering machine.

That world is gone. Today the person calling you has three more tabs open from the same search. When they hit your voicemail, most of them hang up and call the next result. You do not get a message. You do not get a missed-call notification you act on. You get nothing, and critically, **you never learn that it happened.** The job simply goes somewhere else and your phone log shows a call that lasted eight seconds.

This is the option most small businesses are actually using, and it is the most expensive one.

## Option 2: A human answering service

**Cost:** usually per minute or per call, commonly around $1 to $2 a minute, so a real bill that grows with volume. **What it does:** a person answers, takes a message, passes it on. **What it books:** usually nothing, unless you pay for a higher tier.

A good answering service is a genuine upgrade on voicemail. Someone human picks up, the caller feels attended to, and you get a message that a person actually wrote down.

Two structural problems, though.

The first is that most of them take a message rather than solve the problem. The caller wanted an appointment on Thursday. What you get is a note saying they wanted an appointment on Thursday, and now you have to call them back, and now you are playing phone tag with someone who has already called two competitors.

The second is that the pricing works against you. It charges most in your busiest month. Every minute of every wrong number is billable.

Where it genuinely wins: an upset customer, a complicated situation, anything needing real judgment. A skilled human on the phone is still better than any software at those, and it is not particularly close.

## Option 3: An AI receptionist (we call ours a Voice Agent)

**Cost:** a few hundred a month, flat if the vendor prices honestly. **What it does:** answers as your business, 24/7. **What it books:** the actual appointment, during the call.

The difference that matters is not that it sounds human. It is that it can **finish the job.** It reads your live availability, offers real times, books the appointment, sends the confirmation text, and drops a summary in your inbox before you are down the ladder. The caller's problem is solved while they are still on the phone, which is the only moment they are guaranteed to still be your customer.

It also covers the 128 hours a week nobody is at your desk. Nights, weekends, lunch, and every hour you are working.

Where it loses, honestly:

- **Emotional complexity.** A genuinely angry customer, a bereavement, a delicate negotiation. Route those to a person.
- **The unusual call.** Anything outside what it was trained on. A well-built agent will say it does not know and get you involved. A badly built one will make something up, which is worse than voicemail.
- **Your own comfort level.** Some owners hate the idea, and some customers notice. That is a real cost even if it does not show up on an invoice.

## Option 4: A full-time human receptionist

**Cost:** well over $30,000 a year once you count payroll taxes and benefits. **What it does:** everything, well. **What it books:** everything, plus greets people at the door and handles the parts of your business nobody wrote down.

If you have the volume to justify it and the work to keep them busy, a great receptionist is a genuine asset and no software replaces them. The catch is coverage: they work about 40 of the week's 168 hours. The other 128 still need an answer, which is why plenty of businesses with a receptionist also run an AI for nights and overflow.

## How to actually decide

Skip the feature comparison and ask two questions.

**What is one job worth to you?** If your average ticket is in the hundreds or thousands, the arithmetic on anything that answers the phone is easy and you are almost certainly losing more to voicemail than any of these cost. If your average ticket is $20, be much more skeptical.

**What kind of calls do you actually get?** Pull last month's call log. If most calls are "are you open," "how much for X," and "can you come Thursday," an AI handles nearly all of it. If most of your calls are complicated, emotional, or unique, put a human on the phone and use AI only for overflow.
