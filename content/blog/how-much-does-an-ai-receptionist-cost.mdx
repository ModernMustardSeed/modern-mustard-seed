Most pricing pages for AI receptionists are built to prevent you from doing arithmetic. This one is built to let you do it.

Here is what these services actually cost, why the number varies so much, and where the bills hide.

## The four pricing models

Almost every AI receptionist on the market uses one of four structures. The structure matters more than the headline number, because it decides who absorbs the risk of a busy month.

**Per minute.** You pay for talk time, usually somewhere in the range of five to twenty-five cents a minute. This is the vendor passing their own cost straight through to you. It is genuinely cheap when the phone is quiet. The problem is that your busiest month is also your most expensive month, and busy months are when cash is already tight. Unless there is a ceiling, you have signed up for a variable bill you do not control.

**Per call.** Same idea, coarser unit. Easier to forecast than per minute, but you pay full freight for a wrong number and a thirty-second hangup.

**Per seat or per user.** Borrowed from software pricing and largely meaningless for a phone product. Your phone does not care how many employees you have.

**Flat monthly.** One number, every month, regardless of volume. This is the only model where the vendor absorbs the risk instead of you. It is what we use, and it is what I would look for.

## What actually drives the price

Three things, in order of how much they matter.

**How much of the work the vendor does.** This is the real variable. A self-serve tool hands you a dashboard and a prompt box, and you are the one who trains it, tests it, discovers it is telling customers the wrong hours, and fixes it. A done-for-you service does that work and keeps doing it. The gap between those two is most of the price difference in this market, and it is not a scam. It is labor.

**Whether it connects to your real phone number.** Answering on a demo line the vendor gives you is easy. Porting or forwarding your actual business number, the one on your truck and your Google listing, involves telephony work that has to be done correctly. Some vendors charge for it separately. Ask.

**How deep the integrations go.** An agent that takes messages is straightforward. An agent that reads live availability from your calendar, books into it, sends a confirmation text, and drops a summary in your CRM is a real integration project. Priced accordingly.

## The honest comparison to a human

I want to be careful here, because this comparison is usually made dishonestly.

A full-time receptionist in the United States generally costs well north of $30,000 a year once payroll taxes and benefits are counted. That person covers about 40 of the 168 hours in a week. An AI receptionist covers all 168 for a few hundred dollars a month.

That is a real and large advantage, and it is why this category exists. But a human receptionist notices that the caller is crying. They walk the paperwork down the hall. They remember that Mrs. Halvorsen always calls on Tuesdays and asks about her grandson. If your business runs on relationships that deep, the AI is a supplement to your front desk, not a replacement for it.

Where the AI wins outright is the 128 hours a week nobody is at the desk anyway. Nights, weekends, lunch, and every minute you are on a roof or under a sink. Right now those calls go to voicemail, and voicemail is where jobs go to die.

## The only math that matters

Forget the monthly number for a second. Answer this instead: **what is one job worth to you?**

If your average ticket is $400, the receptionist needs to save roughly one job a month to pay for itself. If you are a plumber taking emergency calls at two in the morning, one recovered call can cover a quarter. If you run a restoration company, a single water-damage job can cover a year.

Now the other direction, honestly: if your average ticket is $20 and you get four calls a day, this is a hard sell and you should be suspicious of anyone who tells you otherwise. The category is not right for every business.

The way to find out is not to read more pricing pages. It is to look at your own phone records for last month, count the calls that went unanswered, and multiply by your average ticket. That number is the actual budget. Everything else is a haggle over how much of it you keep.

## Questions to ask any vendor

1. What is my total cost for the first year, in one number?
2. Is there a ceiling on the monthly bill, or can a busy month cost more?
3. Does it answer on my real business number, and is that included?
4. What happens when it does not know the answer to a caller's question?
5. Can I hear it answer as *my* business before I pay anything?

If a vendor cannot answer the fifth one, that is the whole evaluation right there.
